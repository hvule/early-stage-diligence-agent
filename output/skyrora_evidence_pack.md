**Evidence standard:** Facts are identified separately from inferences; absence of evidence is not treated as evidence of absence.

## 1. Founder / Team

### Factual evidence

- Skyrora was founded by **Volodymyr Levykin**, who is also CEO. The company states that he previously operated an IT business in California and served as managing director of an AIM-listed Scottish e-commerce company with more than 600 employees globally.  
  Source: [Skyrora founder biography](https://skyrora.com/personal/who-we-are/volodymyr-levykin/)

- **Dr. Jack-James Marlow** is identified as COO. Skyrora states that he has experience in aerospace engineering, propulsion, combustion research, and holds a PhD in aerospace engineering.  
  Source: [Skyrora 2025 review](https://skyrora.com/quarters-2-4-2025-in-review/)

- Skyrora states that Marlow joined in 2018 after UK Space Agency-funded rocket-engine diagnostics research at Kingston University.  
  Source: [Skyrora 2025 review](https://skyrora.com/quarters-2-4-2025-in-review/)

- The company claims more than **200 years of cumulative rocket experience** across its team. An independent 2025 report described approximately **85 employees plus contractors**, while older company materials referred to more than 100 or 150 employees.  
  Sources: [Skyrora team page](https://skyrora.com/who-we-are/); [NASASpaceFlight](https://www.nasaspaceflight.com/2025/08/skyrora-efis/2/)

### Supporting evidence

- The company has established propulsion, manufacturing, testing, and launch-infrastructure activities in Scotland and elsewhere in Europe.  
  Source: [Skyrora team page](https://skyrora.com/who-we-are/)

- Publicly reported work includes static-fire testing, engine development, additive manufacturing, telemetry, and mobile launch infrastructure. These activities support the existence of a substantive technical organization rather than a purely conceptual venture.  
  Sources: [Skyrora 2020 test report](https://skyrora.com/the-uks-first-complete-ground-rocket-test-in-50-years-takes-place-in-scotland/); [Skyrora telemetry project](https://skyrora.com/skyrora-viasat-and-cgi-partner-to-develop-a-commercial-space-based-launch-vehicle-telemetry-solution/)

### Concerns / contrary evidence

- Founder experience is described primarily as entrepreneurial and operational, rather than as a documented record of successfully developing and commercially operating orbital launch vehicles.
- Public materials do not establish that the senior team has previously taken an orbital launcher through successful flight, certification, and sustained commercial operations.
- Employee counts are inconsistent across company and independent sources. This may reflect growth or different counting methodologies, but the current figure is not independently verified.

### Missing information

- Complete current executive biographies.
- Current board composition and relevant governance experience.
- Detailed backgrounds of propulsion, avionics, flight-safety, launch-operations, and manufacturing leaders.
- Current employee count, contractor count, hiring plan, and technical talent retention.
- Equity ownership and founder incentives.

### Key diligence questions

1. Which executives and technical leaders have prior successful orbital-launch experience?
2. What is the current full-time headcount by function, and how much work depends on contractors?
3. What is the current board and investor governance structure?
4. What percentage of the company is owned by founders, employees, and investors?
5. Which critical engineering roles remain open or dependent on a small number of individuals?
6. What evidence supports the company’s “200 years of cumulative rocket experience” claim?

## 2. Problem Severity & Frequency

### Factual evidence

- Skyrora targets small-satellite manufacturers, Earth-observation companies, scientific payload developers, government customers, and defence users.  
  Source: [Skyrora product and company materials](https://skyrora.com/who-we-are/)

- The company’s stated value proposition is cost-effective and responsive access to space, with emphasis on sovereign UK launch capability.  
  Source: [Skyrora team page](https://skyrora.com/who-we-are/)

- UK parliamentary evidence identified a regional customer ecosystem including Clyde Space, Alba Orbital, and Spire, while describing demand for regular, responsive launch and orbital deployment.  
  Source: [UK Parliament oral evidence](https://committees.parliament.uk/oralevidence/15641/html/)

- NASA reports that 4,577 spacecraft were launched in 2025, with approximately 70% attributable to Starlink; excluding Starlink, 45% of remaining spacecraft were 200 kg or smaller.  
  Source: [NASA SmallSat State of the Art](https://www.nasa.gov/smallsat-institute/sst-soa/integration-launch-and-deployment/)

### Supporting evidence

- Small-satellite operators can face constraints involving launch timing, orbital destination, payload integration, rideshare availability, and dependence on foreign launch providers.
- Government and defence customers may place a higher value on sovereign supply chains, assured access, responsiveness, and mission control than purely commercial customers.
- Dedicated launch is a recognized market category alongside rideshare, orbital-transfer services, and government launch programmes.  
  Source: [NASA SmallSat State of the Art](https://www.nasa.gov/smallsat-institute/sst-soa/integration-launch-and-deployment/)

### Concerns / contrary evidence

- The broader growth in spacecraft launches does not establish equivalent demand for Skyrora’s target segment: dedicated launches to polar or sun-synchronous orbit.
- SpaceX Transporter rideshare pricing starts at approximately **$350,000 for a 50 kg-class payload**, creating a potentially significant substitute for dedicated launch.  
  Source: [NASA SmallSat State of the Art](https://www.nasa.gov/smallsat-institute/sst-soa/integration-launch-and-deployment/)
- No public evidence demonstrates that the named satellite companies have signed paid Skyrora contracts.
- The frequency of customers’ need for dedicated, schedule-controlled launches is not publicly established.

### Missing information

- Number of annual addressable payloads by mass, orbit, geography, and customer type.
- Customer waiting times and frequency of launch delays.
- Quantified cost of missed launch windows or unsuitable orbits.
- Customer willingness to pay for sovereignty, responsiveness, or dedicated service.
- Evidence of defence or government procurement demand at commercial prices.

### Key diligence questions

1. What specific customer failures or costs does Skyrora solve better than rideshare?
2. How many target customers require dedicated launch rather than accepting rideshare?
3. What proportion of target payloads require polar or sun-synchronous orbit?
4. What is the economic value of faster or more responsive launch to customers?
5. Which government or defence missions have budget authorization?
6. Can Skyrora provide customer interviews, pipeline data, or signed commitments validating the problem’s severity and frequency?

## 3. Product / Value Proposition

### Factual evidence

- **Skylark L** is a single-stage suborbital research and technology-demonstration vehicle, approximately 11.65 metres tall, with payload capacity of up to 50 kg and an apogee of approximately 102 km.  
  Source: [Skyrora suborbital vehicles](https://skyrora.com/suborbital-launch-vehicles/)

- **Skyrora XL** is a planned three-stage orbital vehicle intended to carry up to **315 kg** to polar or sun-synchronous orbits. It is specified at 22.7 metres in length and 55,838 kg lift-off mass.  
  Source: [Skyrora XL](https://skyrora.com/product/skyrora-xl/)

- Skyrora XL is designed to use hydrogen peroxide/high-test peroxide and kerosene propellants. The third stage is intended to be restartable and provide orbital-transfer or “space tug” functionality.  
  Source: [Skyrora XL](https://skyrora.com/product/skyrora-xl/)

- The broader offering includes mobile/containerized launch infrastructure, payload integration, dispensers, SkyPrint additive manufacturing, and Ecosene fuel research.

### Supporting evidence

- Skyrora has publicly reported static-fire tests, engine tests, tank and fairing work, mobile launch-complex deployment, and telemetry development.  
  Sources: [Skyrora 2020 test](https://skyrora.com/the-uks-first-complete-ground-rocket-test-in-50-years-takes-place-in-scotland/); [Skyrora telemetry project](https://skyrora.com/skyrora-viasat-and-cgi-partner-to-develop-a-commercial-space-based-launch-vehicle-telemetry-solution/)

- A restartable third stage could expand the service from launch alone to more precise orbital deployment and last-mile delivery.

### Concerns / contrary evidence

- Skyrora has not publicly demonstrated a successful orbital flight or commercial payload deployment.
- Skylark L’s October 2022 launch attempt from Iceland lifted off but did not complete its mission.  
  Source: [Skyrora 2022 launch attempt](https://skyrora.com/skyrora-attempts-first-rocket-launch-to-space-with-icelandic-mobile-spaceport/)
- As of August 2025, Skyrora XL still required further integration and testing; the demonstrator schedule had moved toward early 2027.  
  Source: [NASASpaceFlight](https://www.nasaspaceflight.com/2025/08/skyrora-efis/2/)
- No independent evidence establishes achieved cost, reliability, payload accuracy, or launch cadence.

### Missing information

- Complete technical qualification matrix for each vehicle.
- Flight-test results and failure-root-cause analysis from the 2022 attempt.
- Skyrora XL integrated-stage test results.
- Target launch price, payload pricing, and orbital-transfer pricing.
- Expected reliability and payload insertion accuracy.
- Evidence that Ecosene is flight-qualified and economically competitive.

### Key diligence questions

1. What was the confirmed root cause of the 2022 Skylark L anomaly, and what corrective actions were completed?
2. Which Skyrora XL subsystems have been flight-qualified?
3. What are the planned qualification milestones before the first orbital attempt?
4. What is the target price per launch and price per kilogram?
5. How does the third-stage service compare with independent orbital-transfer providers?
6. What performance penalties arise from using Ecosene or high-test peroxide at orbital scale?

## 4. Why Now?

### Factual evidence

- NASA reports continued growth in small-satellite activity and approximately 10% growth in the 11–600 kg SmallSat segment in 2025.  
  Source: [NASA SmallSat State of the Art](https://www.nasa.gov/smallsat-institute/sst-soa/integration-launch-and-deployment/)
- The UK government has stated an objective of developing a sustainable commercial UK launch market and small-satellite launch capability.  
  Source: [UK Space Agency Annual Report 2024–25](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/uk-space-agency-annual-report-and-accounts-2024-2025)
- The CAA issued Skyrora a Spaceflight Operator Licence dated July 25, 2025.  
  Source: [CAA licence document](https://www.caa.co.uk/data-and-publications/publications/documents/content/ors10-2025-004/)
- ESA launched the European Launcher Challenge, preselecting five competing launch companies in July 2025. Skyrora was not among the five preselected challengers.  
  Source: [ESA European Launcher Challenge](https://www.esa.int/Enabling_Support/Space_Transportation/European_Launcher_Challenge_preselected_challengers_unveiled)

### Supporting evidence

- European governments and ESA appear to be supporting domestic launch capability and supply-chain development.
- Increased satellite deployment, defence demand, and geopolitical sensitivity may increase interest in sovereign or regionally controlled launch infrastructure.
- The existence of licensing and launch-site infrastructure may reduce some barriers for UK launch operators.

### Concerns / contrary evidence

- The same market timing has attracted multiple better-capitalized or more advanced European competitors.
- Rideshare has expanded and may reduce the urgency for dedicated launch.
- Skyrora’s schedule has slipped, potentially allowing competitors to establish flight heritage and customer relationships first.
- Its exclusion from ESA’s five-company European Launcher Challenge shortlist may reduce access to a major institutional demand channel, although the implications are not publicly quantified.

### Missing information

- Current government procurement programmes and committed budgets.
- Whether UK policy support is grant-based, contract-based, or contingent on milestones.
- Comparative timing of Skyrora versus European competitors.
- The extent to which current launch-site capacity is available to Skyrora.
- Quantified effect of geopolitical and defence demand on commercial launch budgets.

### Key diligence questions

1. What government contracts or procurement pathways are currently available to Skyrora?
2. Does the CAA licence materially shorten time to launch, or are substantial approvals still outstanding?
3. Why was Skyrora not selected for the ESA Launcher Challenge?
4. What customer or policy changes make dedicated UK launch more valuable now than three years ago?
5. Which competitors are likely to achieve orbital capability before Skyrora?

## 5. Market & Market-Creation Potential

### Factual evidence

- The broader small-satellite market includes commercial rideshare, dedicated small launch, orbital-transfer services, and government launch programmes.  
  Source: [NASA SmallSat State of the Art](https://www.nasa.gov/smallsat-institute/sst-soa/integration-launch-and-deployment/)
- Skyrora XL’s stated target payload is up to 315 kg into polar and sun-synchronous orbits.  
  Source: [Skyrora XL](https://skyrora.com/product/skyrora-xl/)
- Skyrora has not publicly disclosed a standalone total addressable market, serviceable obtainable market, or expected market share.

### Supporting evidence

Potential market segments include:

- Dedicated launch for payloads that cannot obtain a suitable rideshare orbit or timing.
- UK and European sovereign government missions.
- Defence and responsive-launch missions.
- Suborbital research and technology demonstrations.
- Payload integration and orbital-transfer services.
- Potential licensing or commercialization of propulsion and manufacturing technologies.

- A UK-based launch provider could create demand by enabling customers to design missions around domestic launch availability rather than adapting to foreign rideshare schedules.

### Concerns / contrary evidence

- “Small satellites” is a broad market definition and overstates the directly addressable segment for a 315 kg dedicated launcher.
- SpaceX’s pricing and cadence may constrain the size of the premium dedicated-launch market.
- European launch demand may be fragmented, government-dependent, and insufficient to support multiple launch providers.
- Market creation would require convincing customers to pay for dedicated access rather than merely shifting existing payloads from rideshare.

### Missing information

- Launch demand by payload class and orbit.
- Expected annual missions available in the UK and Europe.
- Customer segmentation and market share assumptions.
- Launch-price benchmarks for Skyrora XL’s target orbit.
- Government anchor-demand estimates.
- Evidence of a market for Skyrora’s suborbital vehicles or third-stage services.

### Key diligence questions

1. What is Skyrora’s bottom-up serviceable market in annual launches and revenue?
2. How many target payloads fall within the vehicle’s mass and orbital envelope?
3. What fraction of the market is expected to pay a dedicated-launch premium?
4. Which government and defence missions are likely to anchor demand?
5. Can orbital-transfer services make rideshare economically complementary rather than competitive?
6. What utilization level is required for a viable launch business?

## 7. Unit Economics & Scalability

### Factual evidence

- Rocket development requires substantial expenditure on propulsion, structures, avionics, testing, launch infrastructure, regulatory compliance, insurance, and skilled labor.
- Skyrora has invested in engine-testing facilities, mobile launch infrastructure, and additive-manufacturing equipment.  
  Source: [Skyrora five-year review](https://skyrora.com/five-moments-for-five-years-a-look-back-at-skyroras-proudest-moments-to-celebrate-half-a-decade-of-operation/)
- Public sources do not disclose vehicle cost, launch price, gross margin, launch-site operating cost, or break-even cadence.

### Supporting evidence

- Additive manufacturing and in-house propulsion may potentially reduce production lead times, tooling requirements, or supply-chain dependency.
- A restartable third stage and payload-integration services could increase revenue per mission.
- A standardized launch vehicle and mobile launch system could, if technically validated, support replication across launch sites.

### Concerns / contrary evidence

- Fixed engineering, regulatory, launch-site, and mission-assurance costs may be high relative to early launch volume.
- There is no public evidence of production yield, manufacturing repeatability, or flight-proven cost reductions.
- A dedicated launcher may have difficulty matching rideshare economics without high utilization or a premium customer segment.
- The company’s cash balance, burn rate, runway, debt, and remaining capex requirement are undisclosed.

### Missing information

- Cost per vehicle and per engine.
- Propellant, labor, integration, insurance, and range costs.
- Planned launch cadence and factory throughput.
- Expected price per launch and gross margin.
- Fixed-cost base and break-even utilization.
- Additional capital required to reach orbital launch and commercial scale.

### Key diligence questions

1. What is the fully loaded cost of a Skyrora XL mission?
2. What are target selling price, gross margin, and contribution margin?
3. What launch cadence is required to reach break-even?
4. Which components constrain production rate?
5. How much additional capital is needed before first orbital launch and before steady-state operations?
6. What cost advantage, if any, is expected from SkyPrint or Ecosene?

## 8. Moat / Network Effects / Disintermediation Risk

### Factual evidence

- Skyrora has developed proprietary or company-specific programmes including SkyPrint additive manufacturing, Skyforce propulsion, Ecosene fuel research, mobile launch infrastructure, and a third-stage orbital-transfer concept.
- The company has established relationships with SaxaVord, ESA, Viasat, CGI, and other engineering or research partners.
- The reviewed sources did not identify a comprehensive public patent portfolio, peer-reviewed technical catalogue, or confirmed freedom-to-operate position.

### Supporting evidence

Potential sources of defensibility include:

- UK regulatory and operational experience.
- Launch-site relationships and accumulated safety-case knowledge.
- Proprietary manufacturing and propulsion processes.
- Sovereign UK supply-chain positioning.
- Flight heritage, once achieved.
- Customer and payload-integration relationships.
- Operational data from repeated launches.

### Concerns / contrary evidence

- Launch technology is capital intensive but not necessarily protected by strong network effects.
- Competitors can potentially replicate vehicle architectures, launch sites, and government-backed supply chains.
- SpaceX and other launch providers may disintermediate dedicated launch through low-cost rideshare and orbital-transfer offerings.
- Customers may prefer proven launch reliability over a national or regional provider.
- The absence of publicly established patent ownership or flight heritage weakens the currently observable moat.

### Missing information

- Patent portfolio, trade secrets, and IP ownership.
- Employment and contractor IP-assignment arrangements.
- Freedom-to-operate analysis.
- Proprietary performance or manufacturing advantages versus competitors.
- Customer switching costs.
- Exclusivity of launch-site or supplier relationships.
- Data advantage from testing or flight operations.

### Key diligence questions

1. Which technologies are patented, proprietary, or protected as trade secrets?
2. Who owns IP developed through ESA, UK government, or university-supported programmes?
3. Are SaxaVord or other launch-site relationships exclusive?
4. What prevents a government customer from using another European launcher?
5. What customer-specific integration assets or switching costs will accumulate?
6. How does Skyrora defend against disintermediation by rideshare plus orbital-transfer providers?

- Each expansion area adds technical, regulatory, commercial, and capital requirements.
- None of the optional businesses has publicly disclosed standalone revenue, margins, customer backlog, or market validation.

### Missing information

- Revenue contribution and investment required by business line.
- Whether SkyPrint or Ecosene will be sold externally or used only internally.
- Customer demand for third-stage services.
- Commercial readiness of Skylark Micro/Nano and Skylark L.
- Economics and regulatory status of Nova Scotia operations.
- Management prioritization and capital allocation across initiatives.

### Key diligence questions

1. Which optional products are currently revenue-generating?
2. Which activities are core strategy versus grant-funded R&D?
3. Can the third stage be used with third-party launchers?
4. What is the expected revenue and margin mix between launch, integration, and in-space services?
5. Does management have sufficient capital and personnel to pursue these initiatives simultaneously?
6. What milestones would justify expanding beyond the core launch vehicle?

## 10. Regulatory / Execution Risk

### Factual evidence

- The UK CAA granted Skyrora a Spaceflight Operator Licence dated July 25, 2025.  
  Source: [CAA licence](https://www.caa.co.uk/data-and-publications/publications/documents/content/ors10-2025-004/)
- Launches remain dependent on site licensing, range control, airspace and maritime coordination, environmental assessments, public-safety analysis, payload approvals, insurance, and international or export-control requirements.
- The CAA consultation material for SaxaVord addressed impact areas, sea recovery, airspace, maritime coordination, and vehicle operations.  
  Source: [CAA consultation material](https://consultations.caa.co.uk/space/skyrora-public-consultation-citizen-space-informat/user_uploads/skyrora-saxavord-aee-v5-1.pdf)
- As of August 2025, Skyrora XL still required first-stage integration and testing, and the demonstrator schedule had shifted toward early 2027.  
  Source: [NASASpaceFlight](https://www.nasaspaceflight.com/2025/08/skyrora-efis/2/)

### Supporting evidence

- Licensing represents meaningful progress and may reduce one regulatory barrier for UK operations.
- Skyrora has conducted multiple ground tests, engine campaigns, suborbital activities, and launch-infrastructure demonstrations.
- The company reported in late 2025 that Skylark L was technically ready for launch and was moving toward flight readiness.  
  Source: [Skyrora 2025 review](https://skyrora.com/quarters-2-4-2025-in-review/)

### Concerns / contrary evidence

- A vehicle operator licence is not blanket authorization for every vehicle, site, payload, configuration, or mission.
- The 2022 Skylark L mission failed to complete its objective.
- There has been no successful orbital flight or commercial payload deployment.
- Schedule changes indicate execution risk and uncertainty around integration and qualification.
- Changing engines or vehicle configurations could trigger additional licensing requirements.  
  Source: [NASASpaceFlight](https://www.nasaspaceflight.com/2025/08/skyrora-efis/2/)

### Missing information

- Confirmed 2026 launch date and payload.
- Status of all site-specific and mission-specific approvals.
- Completion status of Skyrora XL integrated testing.
- Independent reliability assessment and target success probability.
- Insurance, indemnity, liability, and casualty-risk arrangements.
- Root-cause and corrective-action documentation for the 2022 anomaly.
- Current financing runway relative to the launch schedule.

### Key diligence questions

1. What regulatory approvals remain before the next Skylark L launch?
2. Does the existing licence cover the current vehicle, engine, site, trajectory, and payload configuration?
3. What are the gated milestones for Skyrora XL’s first orbital attempt?
4. Has an independent safety and reliability review been completed?
5. What schedule contingency exists for further test failures or licensing delays?
6. What insurance and liability exposure would Skyrora bear per launch?
7. Can the company fund the programme through first orbital flight and subsequent qualification?

## Cross-dimensional evidence gaps

The most consequential unresolved matters span several dimensions:

- **Commercial validation:** no publicly verified paid launch customer, backlog, deposits, or launch-service revenue.
- **Financial capacity:** no audited current revenue, cash balance, burn, debt, valuation, or financing requirement.
- **Technical proof:** no successful orbital flight and no publicly demonstrated integrated Skyrora XL first-stage test.
- **Unit economics:** no disclosed vehicle cost, launch price, gross margin, or break-even cadence.
- **Defensibility:** no comprehensive public patent or freedom-to-operate analysis.
- **Execution timing:** no confirmed 2026 launch date and conflicting public schedule signals.
- **Governance and team depth:** incomplete current executive, board, headcount, and technical-experience information.
