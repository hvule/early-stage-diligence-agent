## Overall challenge

The investor has identified the most important risk—Skyrora has not yet demonstrated orbital flight—but the thesis currently overweights founder pedigree and national narrative while underdeveloping the commercial and financial case.

The key issue is not simply whether Skyrora has “a product.” It is whether it can convert substantial pre-orbital engineering activity into a **reliable, repeatable, insurable, and economically viable launch service with paying customers**. The evidence pack does not yet answer that question.

Also, “everything so far is just mainly conceptual” is too strong. Skyrora has conducted engine, static-fire, manufacturing, telemetry, launch-infrastructure, and licensing work. The more precise criticism is that its **commercially relevant orbital capability remains unproven**.

## 1. Hidden assumptions in the investor’s reasoning

### Assumption 1: Founder aerospace experience is necessary at the CEO level

The thesis treats Levykin’s IT and entrepreneurial background as a major negative. That assumes the CEO must personally possess prior orbital-launch experience.

A more relevant question is whether the company has:

- Experienced propulsion, avionics, flight-safety, and launch-operations leaders.
- A credible chief engineer and independent technical review process.
- The management ability to raise capital, recruit specialists, manage suppliers, and sell to institutional customers.
- Appropriate board oversight and aerospace governance.

The evidence pack does not establish that Skyrora has these capabilities—but neither does it establish that Levykin’s non-aerospace background is itself disqualifying.

### Assumption 2: Lack of “impressive exits” predicts launch-company failure

Prior exits are a weak proxy for success in a capital-intensive, regulated aerospace business. A founder’s absence of notable exits may matter for fundraising credibility, but it does not directly answer whether the company can execute its technical programme.

This is a lower-value concern than:

- Whether the company is adequately financed.
- Whether engineering milestones are being met.
- Whether leadership has previously handled safety-critical hardware.
- Whether the company can sell and repeatedly deliver launch services.

### Assumption 3: Engine testing implies meaningful readiness

The investor appears to give credit for engine testing, but engine tests alone do not establish:

- Integrated-stage performance.
- Guidance, navigation, and control.
- Staging reliability.
- Payload separation.
- Range safety.
- Environmental qualification.
- Orbital insertion accuracy.
- Repeat production quality.

The opposite assumption—“no orbital flight means no meaningful progress”—is also too simplistic. The relevant question is how much of the remaining risk is known, tested, and funded.

### Assumption 4: UK launch leadership creates a viable market

Being one of very few UK or Scottish launch companies may confer policy and identity value, but it does not prove sufficient demand. Sovereignty is valuable only if customers or governments will pay for it at prices that cover the company’s costs.

### Assumption 5: SpaceX rideshare is a direct price comparison

The cited $350,000 price for a 50 kg-class rideshare payload is an important competitive reference, but it is not necessarily an apples-to-apples comparison. The relevant comparison should include:

- Destination orbit.
- Schedule and waiting time.
- Integration costs.
- Insurance.
- Payload constraints.
- Mission assurance.
- Orbit-raising costs.
- Cost of missing a launch window.
- Government or defence requirements.

At the same time, Skyrora cannot simply claim that dedicated launch is better. It must demonstrate that the customer’s incremental value exceeds the price premium.

### Assumption 6: Optional capabilities will translate into multiple businesses

The third stage, SkyPrint, Ecosene, mobile launch systems, and suborbital vehicles are treated as potential optionality. They may instead represent several technically demanding initiatives competing for the same limited capital and personnel.

## 2. Contrary evidence the investor may be underweighting

The investor’s concerns are valid, but the evidence is not purely conceptual.

### Evidence of substantive execution

- Skyrora has built and tested propulsion hardware.
- It has conducted static-fire and ground-testing campaigns.
- It has developed manufacturing and additive-manufacturing capabilities.
- It has worked on telemetry with Viasat and CGI.
- It has deployed or developed mobile launch infrastructure.
- It obtained a UK Spaceflight Operator Licence in July 2025.
- It has relationships involving SaxaVord and institutional partners.
- It has a technical leadership figure, Dr. Jack-James Marlow, with aerospace and propulsion credentials.

These are not equivalent to orbital success, but they are stronger evidence than a purely conceptual company would have.

### Founder concern may be misclassified

The evidence supports concern about **orbital-launch management experience**, not necessarily concern about the founder’s general suitability. Levykin’s business background could be useful for capital raising, organizational development, and commercial management. The diligence gap is the depth and authority of the technical leadership beneath him.

### “About 100 engineers” should not be treated as established fact

The evidence pack refers to inconsistent employee counts, including approximately 85 employees plus contractors in one independent source. The investor should avoid treating “100 engineers” as verified. However, the uncertainty cuts both ways: it may indicate growth, contractor involvement, or inconsistent reporting rather than weakness.

## 3. Alternative interpretations

### No orbital launch could mean either high risk or normal programme stage

The negative interpretation is that Skyrora has failed to convert years of development into flight capability.

An alternative interpretation is that the company has been progressing through the normal high-risk sequence of propulsion, structures, testing, licensing, and integration. The distinction depends on whether milestones are being completed at an acceptable cost and whether the remaining technical risk is bounded.

### The 2022 Skylark L failure could be informative rather than purely negative

The failed mission is a material concern. But a failed suborbital demonstration can also generate valuable design, telemetry, and failure-analysis data—provided the company can demonstrate:

- A confirmed root cause.
- Corrective actions.
- Successful retesting.
- Independent verification that the failure mode is closed.

Without that evidence, the failure remains an unresolved execution signal.

### Dedicated launch may be a niche premium service

Skyrora does not necessarily need to replace rideshare for the whole small-satellite market. It might succeed in a narrower market involving:

- Responsive defence missions.
- Sovereign government payloads.
- Specific polar or sun-synchronous orbits.
- Customers unable to accept rideshare timing.
- Payloads with unusual integration requirements.
- Missions where schedule control has strategic value.

But this alternative requires customer-level evidence of willingness to pay.

### Skyrora XL’s third stage may be complementary to rideshare

The restartable third stage could enable customers to use rideshare for bulk transport and pay Skyrora for more precise final orbital placement. That could enlarge the opportunity.

However, this also means Skyrora may be competing against specialist orbital-transfer vehicles, not merely launch providers. It must show that its third-stage economics and performance are superior to buying rideshare plus an independent space tug.

### The CAA licence is meaningful but not decisive

The licence demonstrates regulatory progress and may reduce one barrier. It does not establish that:

- The current XL configuration is flight-ready.
- The intended mission has all approvals.
- The payload is approved.
- The site and trajectory are cleared.
- The company can obtain insurance at viable prices.
- The licence covers future vehicle or engine configurations.

## 4. Evidence the investor may be overweighting

### 1. National-champion narrative

“First,” “one of very few,” and “UK/Scotland launch capability” are emotionally compelling but not necessarily economically predictive. The key distinction is:

> Sovereign capability may create strategic value, but strategic value does not automatically become commercial revenue.

### 2. Founder background

The founder’s IT history and absence of famous exits are being used as a proxy for execution risk. This may distract from more relevant diligence on the technical team, financing, governance, and programme controls.

### 3. Engine-testing milestone

A thrust-engine test is a useful milestone but is much less probative than integrated vehicle testing, flight qualification, and successful orbital deployment.

### 4. Headcount

A large reported team can be a positive signal, but headcount is not equivalent to capability. It may also increase burn. The investor should ask whether the team is appropriately staffed across:

- Systems engineering.
- Avionics and guidance.
- Flight safety.
- Manufacturing quality.
- Launch operations.
- Regulatory affairs.
- Commercial sales.

### 5. SpaceX as the primary benchmark

SpaceX is relevant as a substitute, but comparing an early-stage European launcher directly to SpaceX can produce an unhelpful binary conclusion. The more relevant comparators are launchers and orbital-transfer providers serving similar payloads, orbits, customers, and price points.

## 5. Evidence the investor may be underweighting

### Commercial validation

This is arguably the largest gap. There is no publicly verified evidence of:

- A paid Skyrora XL launch customer.
- Customer deposits.
- A firm payload manifest.
- Backlog value.
- Launch-services revenue.
- Repeat customers.

Partnerships, grants, technical collaborations, and LOIs are not equivalent to contracted demand.

### Willingness to pay

The thesis says customers may value sovereign and responsive launch, but does not show that they will pay a premium. A customer may prefer UK launch in principle while still selecting rideshare in practice.

### Financial survivability

The investor’s survival probability is not grounded in disclosed:

- Cash balance.
- Monthly burn.
- Debt.
- Remaining capital expenditure.
- Funding runway.
- Cost to first orbital launch.
- Cost of recovering from another failed test.
- Dilution or financing terms.

A technically viable company can still fail through undercapitalization.

### Unit economics and cadence

No evidence is provided for:

- Fully loaded cost per XL mission.
- Selling price.
- Gross margin.
- Factory throughput.
- Launch cadence.
- Break-even cadence.
- Propellant and range costs.
- Insurance and mission-assurance costs.

This is especially important because dedicated launch generally requires sufficient utilization or a substantial premium.

### Trust and reliability friction

A satellite operator entrusting a payload to an unproven launcher faces:

- Payload loss risk.
- Schedule risk.
- Insurance complications.
- Reputational risk.
- Potential replacement-launch costs.
- Regulatory and export-control complexity.

The first successful flight may establish technical credibility, but commercial trust generally requires multiple successful missions and predictable scheduling.

### Competitive timing

Skyrora’s schedule movement toward early 2027 and exclusion from the ESA European Launcher Challenge shortlist may be important competitive signals. They do not prove failure, but they should not be treated as incidental.

## 6. Missing information that could materially alter the thesis

The following evidence could move the assessment substantially in either direction:

### Technical

- Integrated Skyrora XL test results.
- Subsystem qualification matrix.
- Confirmed root cause and closure of the 2022 Skylark L anomaly.
- Independent safety and reliability review.
- Demonstrated staging, guidance, telemetry, and payload-separation tests.
- Target probability of mission success.

### Commercial

- Binding customer contracts versus MOUs and LOIs.
- Deposits, milestone payments, cancellation rights, and launch slots.
- Customer interviews documenting failed rideshare alternatives.
- Willingness-to-pay studies or signed price indications.
- Pipeline conversion rates and sales-cycle duration.
- Expected repeat business from constellations, government, or defence customers.

### Financial

- Cash and runway.
- Historical and projected revenue.
- Monthly burn.
- Debt and grant dependence.
- Remaining capital requirement to first orbital flight.
- Fully loaded mission economics.
- Cost of another test failure or schedule delay.

### Market

- Number of annual payloads within the 315 kg mass/orbit envelope.
- Percentage requiring dedicated launch.
- Government anchor demand.
- Expected utilization at each price point.
- Competitive launch cadence and pricing.

### Defensibility

- Patents and trade secrets.
- IP ownership from government, ESA, and university-funded work.
- Exclusivity of launch-site arrangements.
- Measured cost, schedule, or performance advantage.
- Customer switching costs.

## 7. Potential confirmation bias

There are signs of both positive and negative anchoring.

### Positive anchoring

The investor initially “has a bit liking” because Skyrora is a rare UK/Scottish launch company. This may cause national significance to be confused with investment attractiveness.

The phrase “potential to thrive” appears to be based mainly on being early or distinctive in the UK, rather than on demonstrated customer economics.

### Negative confirmation

After learning that there has been no orbital launch, the investor appears to interpret the whole programme as conceptual. That may cause real development evidence to be discounted too heavily.

A balanced update would distinguish:

- **What has been de-risked:** licensing, ground testing, propulsion work, infrastructure, institutional relationships.
- **What remains unproven:** integrated orbital vehicle performance, reliability, customer demand, economics, and financing.

### Probability compression

The post-evidence probabilities reduce the upper survival estimate from 70% to 50% and eliminate nearly all conditional-sustainability uncertainty, while leaving the best estimate of sustainable-business probability unchanged at 25%.

That may be defensible, but the reasoning does not explain why the evidence should narrow uncertainty without changing the central estimate. It risks creating numerical precision without an explicit probability model.

The first two reflect possible affinity or national-pride bias. ## 9. Potential survivorship or narrative bias

### Narrative bias

The “UK space champion” story may cause the investor to overweight:

- Government support.
- Institutional partnerships.
- Scottish identity.
- Number of engineers.
- The possibility of becoming a national alternative to foreign launch providers.

The counter-narrative—“no orbital launch means conceptual”—is also a simplified story. Rocket development is inherently milestone-based, and the important question is the amount and quality of progress per unit of capital and time.

### Survivorship bias

The investor may be implicitly comparing Skyrora with successful companies that eventually achieved orbital flight, while not considering the much larger population of launch startups that accumulated tests, partnerships, and funding but never reached sustainable operations.

Conversely, using SpaceX as the archetype can create survivorship bias: SpaceX’s eventual success does not imply that a new launcher with a promising technical programme will achieve similar outcomes.

## 10. Structural constraints versus current implementation choices

### Likely structural constraints

These are difficult to eliminate through better execution alone:

- High capital intensity.
- Long development and qualification cycles.
- Regulatory and insurance dependencies.
- Customer reluctance to trust an unproven launcher.
- Strong rideshare economics.
- Potentially limited European demand for dedicated launch.
- Weak natural network effects in launch services.
- Need for repeated reliability and cadence before commercial trust develops.

### Potentially temporary or execution-related constraints

These could improve if the company executes successfully:

- No orbital flight heritage.
- Incomplete XL integration and testing.
- Current schedule slippage.
- Limited disclosed customer traction.
- Inconsistent headcount reporting.
- Lack of public technical documentation.
- Limited commercial proof for optional products.
- Current manufacturing and production-rate uncertainty.

The important diligence question is whether current weaknesses are merely “not yet achieved” milestones or evidence of a programme that is repeatedly missing milestones and consuming capital without proportionate de-risking.

### Strategic choices that may create unnecessary constraints

- Pursuing multiple products before proving XL.
- Operating across multiple jurisdictions and launch sites.
- Building proprietary capabilities that may be expensive relative to outsourcing.
- Developing Ecosene and SkyPrint without clear external demand.
- Emphasizing a dedicated-launch architecture where rideshare plus orbital transfer may be cheaper.

## 11. Whether optionality is transferable or speculative

### Potentially transferable optionality

Some capabilities could plausibly transfer across products:

- Propulsion expertise.
- Avionics and telemetry.
- Additive manufacturing.
- Payload integration.
- Regulatory and launch-site knowledge.
- Mobile launch infrastructure.
- Operational data from flight campaigns.

The third stage could also be useful independently if it can be integrated with third-party launchers.

### Currently speculative optionality

The following should not receive substantial valuation credit without evidence:

- Ecosene as a commercial fuel business.
- SkyPrint as an external manufacturing platform.
- Skylark as a meaningful revenue line.
- Third-stage “space tug” services.
- Nova Scotia or other geographic expansion.
- Licensing of propulsion or manufacturing technology.

Transferability requires more than shared technology. It requires:

- Separate customer demand.
- Independent product economics.
- Regulatory approval.
- Dedicated management capacity.
- Low incremental capital requirements.
- Evidence that the capability is superior to existing suppliers.

There is a risk that optionality is being used to compensate for the lack of proof in the core orbital-launch business.

## 12. Marketplace disintermediation risk

Skyrora faces several forms of disintermediation.

### Rideshare

SpaceX and other providers can continue to improve:

- Price.
- Launch frequency.
- Orbital flexibility.
- Booking convenience.
- Payload integration.

If rideshare becomes sufficiently flexible, only a small premium segment may remain for dedicated launch.

### Rideshare plus orbital transfer

Customers may combine cheap rideshare with an independent orbital-transfer vehicle. This directly challenges Skyrora XL’s potential value proposition, particularly if its restartable third stage is not substantially cheaper or more capable.

### Government procurement substitution

A UK or European government seeking sovereign access could procure from another European launcher, use a government launch programme, or purchase reserved capacity from a foreign provider. Sovereignty may favor Skyrora, but it does not guarantee exclusivity.

### Integration-platform disintermediation

Satellite manufacturers, launch brokers, and mission-integration companies may own the customer relationship and treat Skyrora as an interchangeable transport supplier.

### Trust-based disintermediation

Customers may select proven providers even at a higher price because reliability and schedule certainty are more valuable than geographic proximity. Skyrora’s lack of flight heritage therefore creates both a technical and customer-acquisition disadvantage.

## 13. Five highest-value diligence questions to answer next

### 1. What exact evidence demonstrates orbital readiness?

Obtain the Skyrora XL qualification plan, integrated-stage test results, subsystem maturity matrix, independent safety review, and confirmed closure of the 2022 Skylark L failure. Identify the remaining top technical risks and the pass/fail gates before first orbital flight.

### 2. Who is actually paying, under what terms?

Request a customer-by-customer schedule showing signed contracts, deposits, launch slots, payload mass, orbit, price, cancellation rights, and payment milestones. Separate binding revenue from grants, partnerships, MOUs, and LOIs.

### 3. Do customers have a verified willingness to pay a dedicated-launch premium?

Interview target customers and quantify the cost of rideshare constraints, launch delays, unsuitable orbits, and lack of sovereignty. Determine the price at which they would choose Skyrora over rideshare or rideshare plus an orbital-transfer provider.

### 4. What are the fully loaded economics and required cadence?

Obtain vehicle cost, launch-site cost, labour, propellant, integration, insurance, range, overhead, expected price, gross margin, production throughput, and break-even cadence. Include the effect of a failed launch, schedule delay, and underutilization.

### 5. Can Skyrora finance the path from first flight to repeatable commercial operations?

Review cash, burn, debt, committed funding, remaining capex, financing conditions, and runway under base-case and delay scenarios. The analysis should identify whether the company can survive one or more additional test failures without a financing event that materially changes investor economics.

---

## Bottom line of the challenge

The investor is right to focus on the absence of orbital flight, but the thesis should be sharpened. The central question is not:

> “Has Skyrora launched to orbit yet?”

It is:

> “Has Skyrora converted pre-flight technical progress into a sufficiently de-risked, funded, differentiated, and customer-backed path to repeatable orbital operations?”

The current evidence supports meaningful technical and regulatory progress, but it does not yet establish customer willingness to pay, repeat demand, unit economics, financing sufficiency, or a durable moat. Those are the areas most likely to change the investor’s judgment.
