## 1. Executive summary

### FACT

Skyrora is a privately held UK launch-vehicle developer and launch-services company incorporated in Scotland on **June 23, 2017**. Companies House lists it as active, with its registered office in Cumbernauld, Glasgow. Its latest filed accounts are for the year ended **December 31, 2024**; 2025 accounts were not yet available in the reviewed Companies House record. ([find-and-update.company-information.service.gov.uk](https://find-and-update.company-information.service.gov.uk/company/SC569511))

The company is developing:

- **Skylark L:** a suborbital, single-stage research and technology-demonstration rocket.
- **Skyrora XL:** a three-stage orbital launch vehicle intended to carry up to **315 kg** to polar or sun-synchronous orbits.
- A third-stage orbital-transfer / “space tug” capability.
- Mobile and containerized launch infrastructure.
- Additive-manufacturing and propulsion technologies, including the SkyPrint system and the Ecosene fuel initiative. ([skyrora.com](https://skyrora.com/product/skyrora-xl/?utm_source=openai))

The UK Civil Aviation Authority granted Skyrora a current **Spaceflight Operator Licence** dated **July 25, 2025**. The company describes this as enabling UK launches of Skylark L from SaxaVord Spaceport in Shetland. ([caa.co.uk](https://www.caa.co.uk/data-and-publications/publications/documents/content/ors10-2025-004/))

Skyrora has not yet demonstrated an orbital launch. Its October 2022 Skylark L launch attempt from Iceland lifted off but experienced an anomaly and did not complete the mission. As of August 2025, an independent technical report described Skylark L as awaiting launch and indicated that the Skyrora XL demonstrator schedule had moved from late 2026 toward early 2027. ([skyrora.com](https://skyrora.com/skyrora-attempts-first-rocket-launch-to-space-with-icelandic-mobile-spaceport/?utm_source=openai))

### INFERENCE

Skyrora’s current investment case, if any, is primarily a **development-stage launch infrastructure and sovereign-access-to-space thesis**, rather than a proven commercial launch business. Public evidence supports meaningful engineering progress and regulatory progress, but not yet recurring commercial launch revenue, orbital flight heritage, or a disclosed backlog.

## 2. Official company description and product

### FACT

Skyrora states that it designs, manufactures, and deploys rockets for small-satellite customers. Its stated objective is to provide “cost-effective and responsive access to space,” with a focus on sovereign UK launch capability. The company reports a Glasgow headquarters, a Cumbernauld manufacturing facility, and facilities distributed across Scotland and Europe. ([skyrora.com](https://skyrora.com/who-we-are/?utm_source=openai))

The company’s product architecture includes:

| Product | Publicly stated role | Publicly stated specifications |
|---|---|---|
| **Skylark L** | Suborbital research, technology demonstration, and flight-qualification vehicle | Approximately 11.65 m tall; payload up to 50 kg; apogee approximately 102 km |
| **Skyrora XL** | Orbital small-satellite launch vehicle | 22.7 m long; 55,838 kg lift-off mass; payload up to 315 kg; HTP/kerosene propellants |
| **Third stage / space tug** | In-space orbital insertion and deployment services | Re-ignitable pressure-fed engine; intended to deliver payloads to target orbits |
| **Skylark Micro / Nano** | Suborbital testing, education, atmospheric and engineering experiments | Micro payload capability publicly listed at up to 1 kg |

([skyrora.com](https://skyrora.com/product/skyrora-xl/?utm_source=openai))

The Skyrora XL uses nine first-stage Skyforce engines and one second-stage Skyforce engine, with a smaller third-stage LEO engine. The company publicly specifies a payload capacity of up to 315 kg into polar and sun-synchronous orbits. ([skyrora.com](https://skyrora.com/product/skyrora-xl/?utm_source=openai))

## 3. Founders, executives, and relevant experience

### FACT

**Volodymyr Levykin** is Skyrora’s founder and CEO. According to the company, he previously operated an IT business in California and had been managing director of an AIM-listed Scottish e-commerce company with more than 600 employees worldwide. He later sold his stake and moved to Silicon Valley before returning to Edinburgh to start Skyrora. ([skyrora.com](https://skyrora.com/personal/who-we-are/volodymyr-levykin/?utm_source=openai))

The company identifies **Dr. Jack-James Marlow** as COO, with prior experience in aerospace engineering, rocket propulsion, combustion research, and a PhD in aerospace engineering. Skyrora says he joined the company in 2018 and had previously worked on UK Space Agency-funded rocket-engine diagnostics research at Kingston University. ([skyrora.com](https://skyrora.com/quarters-2-4-2025-in-review/?utm_source=openai))

The company’s 2025 review states that Marlow was appointed COO during 2025 to coordinate engineering, testing, and operations as the company moved toward launch readiness. ([skyrora.com](https://skyrora.com/quarters-2-4-2025-in-review/?utm_source=openai))

Skyrora describes its broader team as having more than 200 years of cumulative rocket experience. An independent 2025 technical article reported a headcount of approximately 85 employees plus contractors, while older company material referred to more than 100 or 150 employees. These figures are not fully consistent and should not be treated as current without direct confirmation. ([skyrora.com](https://skyrora.com/who-we-are/?utm_source=openai))

## 6. Technology and technical development

### FACT

Skyrora XL is designed around hydrogen peroxide / high-test peroxide and kerosene propellants. The company says the third-stage engine can be restarted multiple times and is intended to provide orbital-transfer and deployment services. ([skyrora.com](https://skyrora.com/product/skyrora-xl/?utm_source=openai))

Skyrora has conducted several technology demonstrations and tests, including:

- Static-fire testing of a complete Skylark L vehicle in Scotland in 2020.
- Suborbital launches of smaller Skylark vehicles.
- A 2022 Skylark L launch attempt from Iceland.
- Ground and engine testing of Skyforce propulsion.
- Testing of first-stage engines, tanks, fairings, and launch infrastructure.
- An ESA-supported telemetry demonstration with Viasat and CGI.
- Development of SkyPrint additive-manufacturing equipment.
- Research into Ecosene, an RP-1-equivalent fuel derived from unrecyclable plastic waste. ([skyrora.com](https://skyrora.com/the-uks-first-complete-ground-rocket-test-in-50-years-takes-place-in-scotland/?utm_source=openai))

An independent technical report in August 2025 stated that the nine first-stage 3D-printed engines were nearing completion of their testing campaign, while tank testing and first-stage integration remained ahead. It also reported that Skyrora XL’s target schedule had moved toward early 2027. ([nasaspaceflight.com](https://www.nasaspaceflight.com/2025/08/skyrora-efis/2/))

## 7. Market size and market structure

### FACT

The broader space market is growing, but launch is a narrower and highly competitive segment. NASA’s 2025 SmallSat State-of-the-Art report states that **4,577 spacecraft were launched in 2025**, with approximately 70% attributable to Starlink. Excluding Starlink, 45% of the remaining spacecraft launched had a mass of 200 kg or less. NASA also reports approximately 10% growth in the 11–600 kg SmallSat segment in 2025. ([nasa.gov](https://www.nasa.gov/smallsat-institute/sst-soa/integration-launch-and-deployment/))

The market is structurally divided between:

1. **Large launchers and rideshare:** chiefly SpaceX Falcon 9, with low-cost scheduled rideshare.
2. **Dedicated small launchers:** such as Rocket Lab Electron and emerging European vehicles.
3. **Orbital-transfer and deployment services:** vehicles that provide “last-mile” delivery after rideshare.
4. **Government and defence launch services:** where sovereign access, responsiveness, and assured availability may carry strategic value.

NASA states that SpaceX Transporter rideshare pricing starts at approximately **$350,000 for a 50 kg-class payload**, and notes that rideshare availability has expanded substantially. ([nasa.gov](https://www.nasa.gov/smallsat-institute/sst-soa/integration-launch-and-deployment/))

### INFERENCE

Skyrora’s market opportunity is not simply “all small satellites.” Its serviceable market is more likely concentrated in customers requiring one or more of:

- Dedicated launch rather than rideshare.
- Specific polar or sun-synchronous orbit.
- Responsive or schedule-controlled launch.
- UK or European sovereign supply chains.
- Defence or government assurance.
- Payload handling or orbital-transfer services.

The presence of inexpensive rideshare creates a direct pricing challenge. A dedicated launcher must justify its premium through timing, orbit, sovereignty, payload handling, security, or availability.

### UK market context

The UK government’s stated objective is to develop a sustainable commercial UK launch market and small-satellite launch capability. The UK Space Agency’s 2024–25 annual report references the government’s launch objectives and public support for competing UK launch technologies. ([gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/uk-space-agency-annual-report-and-accounts-2024-2025/uk-space-agency-annual-report-2024-2025?utm_source=openai))

The latest UK space-industry study was published by the UK Space Agency on July 16, 2025, based on research by London Economics. The source confirms the existence of the sector-wide report, but the specific launch-market figures were not extracted from the available page and should be taken from the underlying report before use in a financial model. ([gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/size-and-health-of-the-uk-space-industry-2024))

## 8. Competitors and substitutes

### Direct or near-direct competitors

**Rocket Lab** — Electron is an operational dedicated small-launch vehicle and provides a strong benchmark for launch reliability, cadence, and customer acceptance.

**Isar Aerospace** — Germany-based developer of Spectrum, targeting the small- and medium-satellite market. Isar completed its first orbital test attempt in March 2025, although the mission ended early. ([apnews.com](https://apnews.com/article/5fda4be0c4c5175912d514655080d6e8?utm_source=openai))

**Rocket Factory Augsburg** — German small-launch developer with the RFA ONE vehicle; it has experienced development and test setbacks.

**PLD Space** — Spanish launch company developing the Miura family of vehicles.

**MaiaSpace** — French launcher developer associated with ArianeGroup.

**HyImpulse** — German company developing small-launch vehicles using a hybrid propulsion approach.

**Orbex** — UK competitor developing Prime; the company entered administration in February 2026 after funding difficulties, according to independent reporting. Skyrora stated that it was considering selected Orbex assets. ([theregister.com](https://www.theregister.com/offbeat/2026/02/13/skyrora-eyes-orbex-assets-as-uk-rocket-rival-collapses/4954079?utm_source=openai))

ESA’s European Launcher Challenge shortlisted Isar Aerospace, PLD Space, Rocket Factory Augsburg, MaiaSpace, and Orbex in July 2025. Skyrora was not among the five preselected challengers. ([esa.int](https://www.esa.int/Enabling_Support/Space_Transportation/European_Launcher_Challenge_preselected_challengers_unveiled?rand=771654&utm_source=openai))

### Substitutes

- SpaceX Falcon 9 rideshare / Transporter.
- Rocket Lab rideshare and Electron.
- Government launch programmes.
- Larger European launchers such as Ariane 6 and Vega-C.
- Orbital-transfer vehicles that separate payload deployment from the primary launch provider.
- Delaying launch until a lower-cost rideshare becomes available.
- Air-launch or other responsive-launch concepts.

NASA’s launch-services overview emphasizes that rideshare has become a major access route for small spacecraft and that launch options now include commercial rideshare, dedicated small launchers, dispensers, and government services. ([nasa.gov](https://www.nasa.gov/smallsat-institute/sst-soa/integration-launch-and-deployment/))

### INFERENCE

Skyrora’s sovereign UK positioning may differentiate it from non-UK launchers for certain government and defence missions, but sovereignty alone may not overcome a substantial gap in price, reliability, cadence, or orbital flexibility.

## 12. Regulatory and legal dependencies

### FACT

Skyrora operates under the UK Space Industry Act 2018 framework. The CAA lists a current Spaceflight Operator Licence for Skyrora dated July 25, 2025. ([caa.co.uk](https://www.caa.co.uk/data-and-publications/publications/documents/content/ors10-2025-004/))

Launch operations also depend on:

- Site licences and range-control approvals.
- Airspace and maritime closures.
- Environmental assessments.
- Public-safety and casualty-risk analysis.
- Payload approvals.
- Insurance and indemnity requirements.
- Export-control and national-security rules.
- International coordination where launch trajectories cross or approach other jurisdictions.

The CAA consultation material for Skyrora’s SaxaVord activities addresses impact areas, sea recovery, airspace, maritime coordination, and vehicle operations. ([consultations.caa.co.uk](https://consultations.caa.co.uk/space/skyrora-public-consultation-citizen-space-informat/user_uploads/skyrora-saxavord-aee-v5-1.pdf?utm_source=openai))

### INFERENCE

A vehicle operator licence should not be interpreted as a blanket authorization for every future vehicle or mission. Changes to vehicle configuration, engines, launch site, payload, trajectory, or operating procedures may require additional approvals. Independent reporting also noted that changing engines could trigger a new licensing process. ([nasaspaceflight.com](https://www.nasaspaceflight.com/2025/08/skyrora-efis/2/))

## 13. Major risks and unresolved questions

### Technical and execution risk

- No demonstrated orbital flight.
- The 2022 Skylark L launch attempt failed to complete its mission.
- Skyrora XL still required further first-stage integration and testing as of August 2025.
- Development schedules have shifted materially from earlier public targets. ([skyrora.com](https://skyrora.com/skyrora-attempts-first-rocket-launch-to-space-with-icelandic-mobile-spaceport/?utm_source=openai))

### Commercial risk

- No publicly verified recurring launch revenue.
- No disclosed firm commercial backlog.
- Strong price competition from Falcon 9 rideshare.
- Dedicated launch demand may be smaller than the broader small-satellite population.
- Customer demand may depend heavily on government and defence procurement.

### Financing risk

- Rocket development is capital intensive.
- Public funding is documented, but total private funding and current runway are unclear.
- The company’s 2025 financing, if accurately reported by commercial databases, has not been independently verified in amount or terms.
- Orbex’s collapse illustrates the financing difficulty facing UK launch developers. ([theregister.com](https://www.theregister.com/offbeat/2026/02/13/skyrora-eyes-orbex-assets-as-uk-rocket-rival-collapses/4954079?utm_source=openai))

### Regulatory risk

- A vehicle licence does not eliminate site, payload, insurance, environmental, and range-safety dependencies.
- Additional approvals may be required for Skyrora XL and future engine variants.
- UK and international export-control restrictions could affect payloads, components, and customer eligibility.

### Market-structure risk

- SpaceX’s launch cadence and rideshare pricing exert significant pressure on dedicated small-launch providers.
- European competition is increasing, supported by government and ESA initiatives.
- Potential European consolidation may create better-capitalized competitors.

### Sustainability risk

Skyrora emphasizes Ecosene and plastic-waste-derived fuel, but the public sources reviewed did not provide an independently verified lifecycle assessment, commercial production economics, or evidence that Ecosene is flight-qualified at orbital scale. The environmental benefit therefore remains an area requiring technical and commercial verification.

### Governance and transparency risk

- Public information on ownership, financing, financial performance, and backlog is limited.
- Company-reported employee and schedule metrics are not always consistent across time and sources.
- The distinction between grants, contracts, LOIs, partnerships, and firm customer commitments is not always clear in public communications.
