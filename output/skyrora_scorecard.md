## 1. Founder / Team

- **Score:** 5/10  
- **Confidence:** 68%  
- **Rationale:** Skyrora appears to have a substantive technical organization and experienced aerospace leadership, but the publicly evidenced team lacks a demonstrated record of taking an orbital launcher through successful flight and commercial operations.
- **Strongest positive evidence:** Founder Volodymyr Levykin has prior entrepreneurial and operating experience; COO Dr. Jack-James Marlow has aerospace, propulsion, combustion, and PhD-level expertise. The company reports substantial cumulative rocket experience and has conducted meaningful engineering and testing work.
- **Strongest negative/contrary evidence:** Founder experience is not shown to include successful orbital-launch execution. Current headcount and senior-team depth are inconsistent across sources, and detailed backgrounds for flight-safety, avionics, launch operations, and manufacturing leaders are unavailable.
- **Most important missing evidence:** A current organization chart showing full-time technical leadership, prior orbital-flight experience, contractor dependence, employee retention, board composition, and ownership structure.

## 2. Problem Severity & Frequency

- **Score:** 6/10  
- **Confidence:** 65%  
- **Rationale:** There is a credible need for responsive, sovereign, and orbit-specific launch services, particularly for government and defence users. However, the frequency and willingness to pay for dedicated launch relative to rideshare are not validated.
- **Strongest positive evidence:** Small-satellite activity is growing, and UK evidence identifies demand for regular, responsive orbital deployment. Sovereign access may be strategically important for government and defence customers.
- **Strongest negative/contrary evidence:** SpaceX rideshare pricing and cadence provide a strong substitute. No evidence shows that named target customers require dedicated Skyrora launches or have signed paid commitments.
- **Most important missing evidence:** Customer-level data on launch delays, unsuitable rideshare orbits, mission-window costs, and willingness to pay a dedicated-launch premium.

## 3. Product / Value Proposition

- **Score:** 5/10  
- **Confidence:** 75%  
- **Rationale:** The proposed offering is technically differentiated on paper—particularly the 315 kg orbital vehicle, restartable third stage, mobile launch infrastructure, and integration services—but remains largely unproven in flight.
- **Strongest positive evidence:** Skyrora has conducted engine, tank, fairing, static-fire, telemetry, manufacturing, and mobile-launch activities. The third stage could provide value beyond basic insertion by enabling more precise orbital delivery.
- **Strongest negative/contrary evidence:** No successful orbital flight or commercial payload deployment has been demonstrated. Skylark L’s 2022 mission did not complete its objective, and Skyrora XL still required integration and testing as of the cited 2025 reporting.
- **Most important missing evidence:** Integrated Skyrora XL test results, qualification status by subsystem, root-cause analysis of the 2022 anomaly, expected reliability, insertion accuracy, and target pricing.

## 4. Why Now?

- **Score:** 7/10  
- **Confidence:** 78%  
- **Rationale:** Market and policy timing are favorable: small-satellite deployment is expanding, European sovereign launch capability is receiving institutional attention, and Skyrora has obtained a UK spaceflight operator licence. Timing is nevertheless competitive and may be undermined by schedule slippage.
- **Strongest positive evidence:** NASA reports continued SmallSat growth; the UK government supports domestic commercial launch; and the CAA issued Skyrora a Spaceflight Operator Licence in July 2025.
- **Strongest negative/contrary evidence:** European competition is intensifying, rideshare is improving, and Skyrora was not among ESA’s five preselected European Launcher Challenge companies. Its schedule has also moved toward early 2027 for the demonstrator.
- **Most important missing evidence:** Government procurement budgets, active contract opportunities, remaining approval requirements, and comparative launch timelines against better-funded European competitors.

## 5. Market & Market-Creation Potential

- **Score:** 5/10  
- **Confidence:** 62%  
- **Rationale:** The broader launch and small-satellite markets are substantial, but Skyrora’s directly addressable market is narrower: dedicated missions for payloads up to 315 kg to specific polar or sun-synchronous orbits. Market creation is plausible but not yet quantified.
- **Strongest positive evidence:** Potential segments include dedicated launch, sovereign government missions, defence, suborbital research, payload integration, and orbital-transfer services. A UK provider could enable missions not well served by foreign rideshare.
- **Strongest negative/contrary evidence:** Broad “small-satellite” market statistics overstate the relevant segment. Rideshare and orbital-transfer alternatives may limit the premium dedicated-launch market, while European demand may be fragmented and government-dependent.
- **Most important missing evidence:** A bottom-up serviceable market model showing annual qualifying payloads, target orbits, customer budgets, expected share, pricing, and utilization required for viability.

## 6. Traction Quality & Retention

- **Score:** 3/10  
- **Confidence:** 86%  
- **Rationale:** Skyrora has meaningful technical, licensing, institutional, and infrastructure progress, but little evidence of commercial traction. There is no publicly verified paid orbital-launch customer, backlog, deposit, or launch-services revenue.
- **Strongest positive evidence:** The company holds a UK operator licence, has relationships with SaxaVord and institutional/technical partners, and has completed multiple ground-test and development campaigns.
- **Strongest negative/contrary evidence:** No successful orbital mission, named paid Skyrora XL customer, contracted launch manifest, backlog value, or demonstrated repeat customer behavior is disclosed. LOIs and partnerships may be nonbinding.
- **Most important missing evidence:** Signed customer contracts, deposits, launch-slot reservations, backlog, revenue by business line, pipeline conversion rates, and customer retention or repeat-purchase data.

## 7. Unit Economics & Scalability

- **Score:** 3/10  
- **Confidence:** 89%  
- **Rationale:** The business could theoretically benefit from vertical integration and standardized manufacturing, but there is no public evidence that the launcher has attractive economics or can scale to sufficient cadence.
- **Strongest positive evidence:** In-house propulsion, additive manufacturing, mobile launch infrastructure, payload integration, and a potentially higher-value restartable third stage could improve control of cost, lead time, and revenue per mission.
- **Strongest negative/contrary evidence:** Vehicle cost, selling price, gross margin, production yield, launch cadence, burn rate, runway, and break-even volume are all undisclosed. Dedicated launch economics face direct pressure from low-cost rideshare.
- **Most important missing evidence:** Fully loaded mission cost, target price and margin, fixed-cost base, factory throughput, production constraints, break-even cadence, and capital required through first orbital flight and commercial scale.

## 8. Moat / Network Effects / Disintermediation Risk

- **Score:** 4/10  
- **Confidence:** 67%  
- **Rationale:** Skyrora may develop defensibility through flight heritage, regulatory know-how, proprietary manufacturing, and customer integration experience, but the current observable moat is limited and network effects are weak.
- **Strongest positive evidence:** The company has company-specific programmes such as SkyPrint, Skyforce, Ecosene, mobile launch infrastructure, and an orbital-transfer concept, alongside relationships with launch sites and institutional partners.
- **Strongest negative/contrary evidence:** No comprehensive public patent portfolio, freedom-to-operate analysis, or flight heritage is established. Competitors may replicate architectures, while rideshare plus orbital-transfer services could disintermediate dedicated launch.
- **Most important missing evidence:** Patents and trade secrets, IP ownership, exclusivity of launch-site relationships, independently validated cost or performance advantages, and customer switching costs.

## 9. Optionality & Expansion

- **Score:** 6/10  
- **Confidence:** 70%  
- **Rationale:** Skyrora has several plausible adjacencies across suborbital launches, orbital launch, payload integration, space tugs, manufacturing, propulsion, and alternative fuels. However, most are unvalidated and could increase capital needs and management complexity.
- **Strongest positive evidence:** The restartable third stage could support orbital-transfer services, while SkyPrint, Ecosene, mobile launch infrastructure, and suborbital vehicles create potential non-launch revenue streams.
- **Strongest negative/contrary evidence:** No standalone revenue, margin, backlog, or customer validation is disclosed for these activities. Pursuing multiple initiatives before proving the core orbital vehicle could dilute focus.
- **Most important missing evidence:** Revenue and investment by business line, commercial readiness of Skylark products, demand for third-stage services, external sales potential for SkyPrint/Ecosene, and management’s capital-allocation priorities.

## 10. Regulatory / Execution Risk

- **Score:** 3/10  
- **Confidence:** 88%  
- **Rationale:** Licensing progress is meaningful, but launch execution remains highly uncertain. The company has no demonstrated orbital success, has experienced a prior suborbital mission failure, and still faces vehicle, site, payload, safety, insurance, and schedule dependencies.
- **Strongest positive evidence:** Skyrora obtained a CAA Spaceflight Operator Licence and has conducted extensive ground testing, engine campaigns, launch-infrastructure work, and regulatory engagement.
- **Strongest negative/contrary evidence:** A licence is not blanket approval for every vehicle or mission. The 2022 Skylark L attempt failed, Skyrora XL remained in integration/testing, and public schedules have shifted. There is no independent reliability assessment or confirmed orbital-launch date.
- **Most important missing evidence:** Current mission-specific approvals, integrated qualification results, independent safety review, anomaly root-cause closure, insurance and liability terms, launch schedule contingencies, and financing runway through orbital qualification.
