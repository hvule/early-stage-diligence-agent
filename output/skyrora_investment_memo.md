# Skyrora — Investment Diligence Memo

## 1. Executive view

Skyrora has made respectable progress in propulsion testing, manufacturing, launch infrastructure, institutional partnerships, and UK licensing. The company appears to have a substantive technical organization rather than being purely conceptual.

However, its commercially relevant orbital capability remains unproven. Skyrora has not completed a successful orbital flight or demonstrated paid, repeatable launch demand. Technical readiness, customer willingness to pay, unit economics, financing runway, and defensibility remain insufficiently evidenced.

**Agent Assessment:** High-risk, pre-orbital launch company with meaningful development progress but weak commercial validation and substantial execution risk.

**Investor Judgment:** Need more information. **Not Pass.**

---

## 2. Core thesis

The investment case depends on Skyrora converting pre-flight engineering work into a reliable, repeatable, insurable, and economically viable launch service.

The investor recognizes potential upside if the company survives the next three years and achieves commercial operations, but remains hesitant because:

- No successful orbital flight has been demonstrated.
- The 2022 Skylark L mission did not complete its objective.
- Founder aerospace-launch experience and prior exits are not established.
- Skyrora’s technological advantages over competitors such as other European launch providers and SpaceX are unclear.
- Paid customer demand, backlog, launch pricing, margins, and capital requirements are undisclosed.
- The company’s patents, proprietary technology, and aerospace differentiation have not been sufficiently disclosed.

## 4. What changed through diligence

Diligence strengthened the positive case by confirming that Skyrora has:

- Conducted engine, static-fire, and ground-testing campaigns.
- Developed additive manufacturing and mobile launch infrastructure.
- Worked on telemetry with Viasat and CGI.
- Established relationships with SaxaVord and institutional partners.
- Obtained a UK Spaceflight Operator Licence in July 2025.

Diligence also reinforced the principal concerns:

- No successful orbital flight or commercial payload deployment.
- No publicly verified paid Skyrora XL customer, deposits, backlog, or launch-services revenue.
- The 2022 Skylark L failure remains important absent clear public evidence of root-cause closure.
- Skyrora XL still required integration and testing, with the demonstrator schedule moving toward early 2027 in cited reporting.
- No disclosed unit economics, financing runway, or required capital to reach repeatable operations.
- No clear public evidence of a durable technology or IP moat.

The investor’s concern that the company is “mainly conceptual” should be refined: Skyrora has completed substantial pre-orbital work, but its **commercially relevant orbital capability remains unproven**.

## 5. Bull case

If Skyrora successfully completes qualification and orbital flight:

- UK and European governments may value sovereign launch capacity.
- Defence and responsive-launch customers could support a premium segment less exposed to standard rideshare.
- Skyrora XL’s stated 315 kg polar or sun-synchronous capability could address missions poorly served by rideshare.
- A restartable third stage could support orbital-transfer or “space tug” services.
- In-house propulsion, additive manufacturing, and mobile launch infrastructure could improve supply-chain control and scalability.
- Flight heritage could create credibility, customer trust, and a stronger competitive position.
- The business could generate substantial profits if it survives the development phase and achieves sufficient launch cadence.

**Investor Judgment:** There may be enormous profit potential if the company survives beyond three years and reaches sustainable operations.

---

## 6. Bear case

The principal downside risks are:

- Further technical failures or integration delays prevent orbital flight.
- The company requires additional capital before achieving commercial milestones.
- Rideshare, particularly SpaceX, remains cheaper and increasingly flexible.
- Customers may prefer proven providers despite the strategic appeal of UK or European sovereignty.
- Dedicated-launch demand may be too small or too government-dependent to support viable utilization.
- Launch insurance, safety, regulatory, and mission-assurance costs may undermine margins.
- Optional initiatives such as SkyPrint, Ecosene, and orbital-transfer services may dilute capital and management focus.
- Competitors may achieve flight heritage first and capture customer relationships.
- The absence of a clear IP or performance moat may leave Skyrora vulnerable to disintermediation.

---

## 7. Unresolved questions

The highest-priority diligence questions are:

1. What integrated Skyrora XL tests have been completed, and what technical gates remain before orbital flight?
2. What was the confirmed root cause of the 2022 Skylark L failure, and how has it been independently closed?
3. Which customer relationships are binding contracts with deposits or committed launch slots?
4. What is the current paid backlog and expected launch-services revenue?
5. What evidence shows customers will pay a premium over rideshare?
6. What are the fully loaded mission cost, target selling price, gross margin, and break-even cadence?
7. How much cash and runway remain through first orbital flight and repeatable commercial operations?
8. Which technologies are patented or protected as trade secrets, and what is Skyrora’s freedom-to-operate position?
9. What is the current headcount by function, including contractor dependence and technical leadership depth?
10. Why was Skyrora not selected for ESA’s European Launcher Challenge, and what alternative institutional demand is available?
